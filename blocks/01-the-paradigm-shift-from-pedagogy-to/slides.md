layout: Caption
title: The default trap
content:
  type: ol
  color: accent1
  items:
    - Didactic lecture
    - High-stakes exam
    - Clinical rotation
    - "*Watch me do it*"
    - "*Now you do it*"
caption: "**The pedagogical default:** treating adult professionals like dependent novices."
notes: |
  Veterinary education traditionally relies on pedagogy. While we often
  associate the word 'pedagogy' with children, Malcolm Knowles used it to
  describe any learning situation where the student is dependent and the
  teacher holds all the knowledge.

  In the pre-clinical years, this works perfectly. The professor holds the
  knowledge, the student is a dependent receiver, and the motivation is
  entirely external - passing the board exams.

  But what happens when that fourth-year student steps into your ICU? The
  clinical context shifts immediately, but often, our teaching style does
  not. We default to what I call the 'Mini-Me' approach. Driven by patient
  safety and acute time constraints, we rapidly demonstrate a procedure and
  expect the student to replicate it. We are treating an adult, autonomous
  professional like a dependent novice. We are using pedagogy in a space
  that desperately requires andragogy.

--- slide
id: ap01-03
layout: Comparison
title: Enter Knowles — the andragogical shift
left_head: "Pedagogy: subject-centred"
left:
  type: p
  color: accent2
  text: |
    *"What will be on the test?"*

    The teacher holds the knowledge. Motivation is extrinsic. The learner
    is a dependent receiver.
right_head: "Andragogy: problem-centred"
right:
  type: p
  color: accent3
  text: |
    *"How will this help me save this patient?"*

    The learner is self-directing. Motivation is intrinsic. They need to
    know **why** before they invest.
notes: |
  Malcolm Knowles posited that adults learn fundamentally differently than
  children. They are self-directing. They don't want to memorize for the
  sake of memorization; they need to know why they must learn something
  before they invest the energy to learn it.

  This is your Zone of Proximal Convergence intersection. The clinical
  environment is inherently andragogical. It is problem-centered, not
  subject-centered.

  When a student is in your clinic, they aren't learning about
  immune-mediated hemolytic anemia to pass a pharmacology test. They are
  learning it because a dog is crashing in front of them right now. The
  motivation shifts from extrinsic - grades - to intrinsic - saving a life
  and professional identity.

  VISUAL: a balance scale, pedagogy pan heavy and down, andragogy pan light
  and rising.

--- slide
id: ap01-04
layout: Comparison
title: The clinical application
left_head: "Directive — builds a technician"
left:
  type: p
  color: accent2
  text: |
    *"Today we are learning about feline urethral obstruction. Here is the
    pathophysiology. Watch me unblock the cat. Now you try."*
right_head: "Problem-centred — builds a diagnostician"
right:
  type: p
  color: accent3
  text: |
    *"This cat has a urethral obstruction. Based on your clinical pathology
    knowledge, what is happening to this cat's potassium levels? What is
    our first step, and why?"*
notes: |
  Let's apply this directly to the clinic floor. Look at the pedagogical
  approach on the left. It is subject-centered. It treats the student as an
  empty vessel. It is fast, I'll grant you, but it doesn't build clinical
  reasoning.

  Now look at the andragogical approach on the right. It honors the
  student's prior pre-clinical knowledge. It asks them to reason through the
  problem. It shifts the educator from 'Holder of Knowledge' to 'Facilitator
  of Reasoning.'

  Yes, asking 'why' takes a few extra seconds compared to just telling them
  what to do. But the andragogical approach builds a diagnostician, not a
  technician. You are leveraging their existing knowledge to bridge the gap
  to clinical competence.

  VISUAL: two contrasting speech bubbles, left outlined in the warning
  accent, right in the affirmative accent.
